Before and after class, use this interactive web app to build intuition for two core pillars of statistical inference: the Sampling Distribution of the Sample Mean \(\overline{X}\) and the Sampling Distribution of the Sample Proportion \(\overline{p}\).

Key Big Ideas You Will Experience Here:
1. \(\overline{X}\) is a random variable with \(\mu_{\overline{X}} = \mu\) and \(\sigma_{\overline{X}} = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) (or \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\dfrac{N-n}{N-1}}\) when \(\dfrac{n}{N} > 0.05\)).
2. \(\overline{p}\) is a random variable with \(\mu_{\overline{p}} = p\) and \(\sigma_{\overline{p}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\), approximately Normal when \(np \ge 5\) and \(n(1-p) \ge 5\).

Theorem 1.1 (Always True for Independent Samples):
If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are drawn independently from any distribution with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\): \[\mu_{\overline{X}} = \mu, \qquad \sigma_{\overline{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\] Crucial Remark (Slide 3): \(\overline{X}\) is a random variable (it varies with each sample); \(\mu_{\overline{X}}\) and \(\sigma_{\overline{X}}\) are fixed numbers.

3. Small Population Effect: Finite Population Correction (FPC) Slides 10–12 · Ex 1.6

In practice, we usually sample without replacement from a finite population of size \(N\). Rule of Thumb (Slide 11): if \(\dfrac{n}{N} \le 0.05\), use \(\sigma_{\overline{X}} = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\); if \(\dfrac{n}{N} > 0.05\), multiply by the finite population correction factor:

\[\sigma_{\overline{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \times \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}} \qquad \left(\text{when } \frac{n}{N} > 0.05\right)\]

Real-World Reminder (Slide 12): Don't Forget Non-Sampling Errors! (1984 US Presidential Poll Story)

Even a large sample cannot fix a biased sampling method. In a 1984 US presidential test poll, stopping after calling subjects who answered on the first try gave Reagan only a 3-point lead over Mondale. When interviewers persisted—calling some randomly selected subjects up to 30 times—Reagan showed a 13-point lead, much closer to the actual election result (Republican voters were simply less likely to be at home!).

  • Make the sample truly random (avoid convenience/non-response bias).
  • Keep questionnaires clear and prevent intentional false responses.
  • Double-check data entry into computers.

1. From Sample Mean \(\overline{X}\) to Sample Proportion \(\overline{p}\) Part 2 · Ex 2.1

Why \(\overline{p}\) Is Just a Special Case of \(\overline{X}\)!

When each sampled item has only two outcomes — Success (1) with probability \(p\) or Failure (0) with probability \(1-p\) — the total number of successes is \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\), and the sample proportion is:

\[\overline{p} = \frac{X}{n} = \frac{Y_1 + Y_2 + \dots + Y_n}{n}, \qquad Y_i \in \{0, 1\}\] \[\mu_{\overline{p}} = p, \qquad \sigma_{\overline{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\] (If sampling without replacement from a finite population with \(n/N > 0.05\), multiply \(\sigma_{\overline{p}}\) by \(\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}\).)
Normal Approximation Rule of Thumb:
By CLT, \(\overline{p} \approx N\!\left(p,\, \dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}\right)\) is reliable whenever both: \[np \ge 5 \qquad \text{AND} \qquad n(1-p) \ge 5\]

Why Does Continuity Correction (\(\pm 0.5\)) Help?
Each discrete Binomial bar at integer \(k\) spans from \(k - 0.5\) to \(k + 0.5\). Without continuity correction, a continuous Normal curve cuts straight through the middle of the endpoint bar, missing half of \(P(X = k)\)!
